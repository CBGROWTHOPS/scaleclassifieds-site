1d:T1aa0,<p><em>Names and a few details are changed at the marketer&#39;s request. The numbers are his. His full setup is now <a href="/course">a $47 course</a>, credited back on your first posting order.</em></p>
<p>Marcus had been running affiliate offers for three years the usual way: buy traffic on Facebook or TikTok, send it to a bridge page, hope the payout beats the ad spend. By spring he was tired of watching campaigns die the moment an ad account got flagged. He wanted a traffic source that didn&#39;t depend on an algorithm liking him that week.</p>
<p>He found it in the least glamorous place on the internet: the <strong>gigs</strong> section of Craigslist.</p>
<h2>The offer</h2>
<p>Marcus promoted rewards apps — the kind that pay people a few dollars to try a mobile game, finish a survey, or hit a level. His network paid him roughly <strong>$4.50 per completed signup</strong> on the best one. Not a lot per lead, which is exactly why paid social never worked for him: a $4.50 payout can&#39;t survive a $6 cost per lead.</p>
<p>Craigslist changed the math. A gigs post in a mid-size metro cost him <strong>$7 in Craigslist fees plus $15 to have it posted for him</strong> — $22 all-in — and it stayed live for 30 days. If a single post produced five signups over its life, he broke even. Anything above that was profit, and the good posts produced a lot more than four.</p>
<h2>Why gigs, and why computer gigs</h2>
<p>The gigs section is where people go looking for small, flexible ways to make money: a day of labor, a focus group, a few hours of data entry. Someone scrolling <em>computer gigs</em> at 11pm is already in the exact frame of mind a rewards app needs. Marcus&#39;s ad read like the listings around it — a plain title, a plain description of what the task was and what it paid, a link to get started. No hype, no emojis, no &quot;make $5,000 a month from home.&quot;</p>
<p>That restraint mattered more than he expected. Craigslist readers flag anything that smells like a scheme, and flagged posts disappear. Ads that looked like an honest gig stayed up.</p>

<p>Averaged across every post, that&#39;s ten signups and about $23 of profit per post. The average hides the real story, though, which was in the spread between cities.</p>
<h2>The metros that worked, and the ones that didn&#39;t</h2>
<p>His first instinct was to hit the biggest markets — New York, Los Angeles, the Bay Area, Miami. They produced signups, but Craigslist charges its highest gigs fee there ($10), and the section is crowded with competing posts, so each ad got pushed down the page fast.</p>
<p>The winners were the tier below: <strong>Phoenix, Sacramento, the DC suburbs, San Diego, Minneapolis, Austin, Charlotte, Detroit.</strong> Cheaper posting fee, plenty of readers, far fewer competing ads. His best metro returned nearly double what he spent on it. A handful of markets — Atlanta, Columbus, Portland — never got above break-even and he cut them by week four.</p>
<p>He also learned that posting daily beat posting in bursts. Craigslist&#39;s search is sorted by time; a steady drip kept one of his posts near the top of the section every day instead of ten of them at once followed by nothing.</p>
<h2>The part that almost sank it</h2>
<p>Posting 900 ads by hand across 40 cities is not a weekend project. Craigslist allows one post per category per city every 48 hours, phone-verifies accounts, and quietly hides (&quot;ghosts&quot;) posts from accounts that behave like bulk posters. Marcus&#39;s first attempt at doing it himself ended after eleven days with two dead accounts and a week of posts nobody ever saw.</p>
<p>The fix was outsourcing the posting. He kept writing the ads and picking the cities; a posting team with their own verified accounts and their own numbers put the ads up, one per city per day, and sent him the live links. His cost went up by fifteen dollars a post. His time went from thirty hours a week to about three.</p>
<h2>What the posting side actually looks like</h2>
<p>Marcus never touches Craigslist now. He writes the ad, picks the cities, and the posting happens somewhere else. This is that somewhere else, in about ninety seconds: the ad going in, twenty five cities being added, and the live links coming back.</p>
